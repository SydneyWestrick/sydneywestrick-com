Your client has $3 million sitting in a brokerage account.

They need $800,000 for a real estate purchase.

You tell them their options are to sell shares, take out a personal loan, or wait.

None of those answers are wrong. But they're all incomplete.

There's a fourth option most advisors either don't mention or can't explain clearly enough to make it land: **securities-backed lending**.

Today's issue is a ground-up breakdown of exactly what it is, how it works, and what your clients actually need to understand before they consider it.

## SBL 101: the plain-language breakdown

### What it is — in one sentence

Securities-backed lending is a loan in which your investment portfolio serves as collateral. You borrow against what you own — without selling it. The portfolio stays invested. You get cash. The lender holds a lien on the pledged assets until the loan is repaid.

### Why does it exist?

Selling a position to access cash has a cost most clients don't fully price in: capital gains taxes, loss of market exposure, and the friction of re-entering the position later. SBL was built to solve that problem. You keep the position — and its upside — while unlocking the liquidity you need. The portfolio continues to appreciate and collect dividends while the loan is outstanding. You're not exiting. You're leveraging.

### How much can you actually borrow?

This is where most clients have unrealistic expectations in both directions. The amount you can borrow is expressed as a loan-to-value ratio — LTV — which is the loan amount as a percentage of the collateral's market value. The LTV you qualify for depends entirely on what you're pledging.

Here's what the market actually looks like:

- **Investment-grade bonds:** 80–90% LTV. The most stable collateral gets the highest advance rate.
- **Large-cap equities** — S&P 500, blue-chip names: 65–75% LTV.
- **Diversified ETFs and mutual funds:** 60–75%, depending on what's underneath.
- **High-yield bonds:** 50–70%. The spread risk earns a discount.
- **Mid-cap equities:** 50–65%. Higher volatility means a lower advance rate.
- **Concentrated single-stock positions:** 30–50%. Single-stock risk is real, and lenders price it accordingly.

### What qualifies — and what doesn't

Most advisors assume more clients are disqualified than actually are. Eligible collateral includes blue-chip and large-cap stocks, diversified equity portfolios, investment-grade bonds, and liquid ETFs and mutual funds. What doesn't qualify: penny stocks under $5, thinly traded or illiquid securities, and most cryptocurrencies. Private equity is evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

The practical implication: a typical HNW client with a diversified public-equity portfolio almost certainly has collateral that is pledgeable. The conversation is worth having.

### The one structural question every client asks: what happens if the market drops?

This is the margin call question, and it needs a direct answer. If the value of the pledged collateral falls significantly, the lender can issue a margin call — requiring the borrower to either post additional collateral or repay part of the loan on short notice. This is the primary risk in any SBL structure, and it should be stress-tested before the facility is opened, not after.

The way to manage it: build in a collateral buffer by borrowing well below the maximum LTV, model the drawdown scenario before committing, and ensure the client understands the cure timeline if a margin call comes in.

### What it costs

SBL facilities are typically interest-only with no fixed amortization schedule — meaning the borrower pays interest on the outstanding balance and repays principal on their own timeline. Most facilities carry variable rates tied to a benchmark like SOFR, which means the cost moves with the rate environment. There's also an important tax note: interest expense deductibility depends on what the proceeds are used for and varies by jurisdiction. That's a conversation for tax counsel, but it should happen before closing, not after.

## Moving forward

SBL is not the right answer for every client. It's the right answer for clients who have pledgeable assets, a clear use for the capital, and an honest understanding of the risk of margin calls. Bridge financing, a real estate purchase, and a tax-planning window around a liquidity event — these are the use cases where the structure genuinely earns its place.

### Worth noting

- The best SBL use cases: bridge financing, real estate purchases, and tax planning around liquidity events. The worst: speculative leverage on top of an already volatile position.
- Always stress-test the facility against a 20–30% portfolio drawdown before your client signs anything.

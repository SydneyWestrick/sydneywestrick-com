## The concentrated stock problem

Wealth advisors managing HNW and UHNW clients encounter the same situation again and again. A founder holds the majority of personal net worth in a single equity position. A pre-IPO employee accumulates restricted stock that vests faster than diversification permits. A senior executive receives ten years of stock-based compensation that now dwarfs every other line item on the balance sheet.

Conventional wisdom prescribes diversification. Sell the concentrated position, redeploy across asset classes, capture the risk-adjusted return improvement that decades of portfolio theory document. The advice is correct on paper and often impossible in practice. The sale triggers a tax event the client is unwilling to accept. The position carries restrictions that prevent disposal. The founder has emotional or strategic reasons to maintain ownership. The position represents control rights or signaling value that cannot be replaced.

Securities-backed lending sits in the gap between the textbook answer and the practical constraint. It allows the client to access liquidity collateralized by the concentrated position without forcing the sale.

<div class="callout-def">
<span class="def-label">Definition · Concentrated Stock Position</span>
A concentrated stock position is generally defined as a single-issuer equity exposure exceeding 10% of total investable assets, though the threshold varies by advisor and institution. Many UHNW situations involve concentrations of 50% or more. The relevant question is not the percentage but whether reduction is feasible.
</div>

## Why SBL solves what diversification cannot

SBL replaces the binary "sell or hold" choice with a third option: borrow. The client pledges the concentrated position as collateral, receives a line of credit at LTV ratios typically between 30% and 50% for single-issuer positions, and uses the proceeds for the underlying need — tax payments, real estate, business expansion, lifestyle, or to fund a true diversification program over time rather than in a single tax-triggering transaction.

The position remains in the client's name. Voting rights, dividend rights, and economic exposure all remain intact. The cost basis stays at its original level. The wash sale rule does not engage because no sale occurred.

### The strategic value beyond liquidity

Three structural advantages compound when SBL is used thoughtfully on concentrated positions. First, capital that would have been lost to taxation remains in the position, continuing to generate returns. For a founder with a 10x basis-to-market gain, the tax friction of selling can exceed 30% of position value. SBL preserves that capital at the cost of the loan spread.

Second, SBL creates optionality on the timing of any future sale. The client can deleverage and sell across multiple tax years, sell during favorable rate or valuation environments, or never sell at all and pass the position to heirs at a stepped-up basis.

Third, SBL preserves the client's posture relative to the position. Founders who sell signal to markets, employees, and acquirers. Founders who borrow signal nothing.

## LTV mechanics for single-issuer collateral

Loan-to-value ratios on concentrated positions are systematically lower than on diversified portfolios. A diversified portfolio of S&P 500 holdings might receive 70% LTV. A single concentrated position will typically receive 30-50%, with the exact ratio determined by issuer-specific factors.

Four factors drive the LTV negotiation. Market capitalization, where larger issuers receive higher advance ratios. Average daily trading volume, where higher liquidity supports higher ratios. Realized volatility over rolling 30 and 90 day windows, where lower vol supports higher ratios. And the borrower's overall relationship with the institution, which can move the ratio by 5-10 percentage points either direction.
